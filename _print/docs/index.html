1 - Overview

A quick overview of Meshtastic and how it works

Meshtastic is an open-source project for communicating over long distances without relying on cellular service or Wi-Fi. Affordable devices use LoRa radio technology to form a mesh network that can relay text messages and share GPS locations, even in remote areas. It can help people stay connected during outdoor activities, emergencies, and local events when other communication methods are unavailable.

2 - FAQs

Answers to common questions about coverage, messages, privacy, and getting connected

What is Meshtastic, in plain English?

Think of text messaging over small radios. Your phone connects to your radio, which exchanges messages with nearby nodes. Other nodes can relay messages farther across the mesh. Both ends need compatible settings and a working radio path.

Start with Meshtastic Basics and our getting-started guide.

Do I need cell service, internet access, or a license?

You don’t need cellular service or internet access for radio-to-radio messaging. Install the app and set up your node before going offline. A phone typically connects to its nearby node over Bluetooth; LoRa carries messages between radios.

You don’t need an amateur radio license for normal BayMesh participation with suitable US-region hardware used within its applicable limits. Leave Licensed Operator off, even if you have a call sign. See Licensed Operations for the difference.

Why don’t I see any nodes or receive messages?

Check your settings, then your node’s placement:

  1. Compare your radio with our recommended settings: US region, Medium Fast, frequency slot 45, and the default public primary channel. Factory settings may use a different preset.
  2. Check that the radio is powered, the app is connected to the right device, and an appropriate 915 MHz antenna is attached if your model needs one. Turn the radio off before changing antennas.
  3. Try a higher, clearer location. A window or outdoor spot can help; walls, hills, trees, and metal can block the path. Test away from your body and other electronics.
  4. Allow time to hear traffic, then send one short introduction or arrange a test with someone nearby. Repeated test messages can add congestion without fixing the underlying problem.
  5. Check the maps and ask in Discord. A map is a clue, not a coverage guarantee.

If your app offers local mesh discovery, it can help identify nearby activity. Follow BayMesh’s settings for the community network; settings from another city’s guide may put you on a different mesh. See the official joining guide.

What does “acknowledged” mean? Did someone read my message?

A successful channel message usually means another node heard and rebroadcast it. It does not prove that everyone on the channel received it. A direct-message acknowledgment confirms that the destination node received it, but not that a person read it.

A missing acknowledgment can mean the message or its return acknowledgment was lost. It doesn’t always mean nobody received it. For an important check-in, ask the person to reply. See the official message-status explanations.

How far will my node reach?

No product listing can tell you how far your node will reach. A clear path from an elevated site can reach much farther than two radios separated by buildings or a ridge. Mesh relays help only when a chain of nodes has compatible settings and working radio paths.

Test the routes you care about. Improve placement before buying more equipment, and ask nearby members what works in your area.

It works outside, but not inside my house. What can I do?

A second node in a higher, clearer location can relay messages to your indoor or portable node. Start with Client for the home node and consider Client Mute for an additional nearby handheld, following our role recommendations.

For a more tailored setup, supported firmware offers Client Base, which prioritizes relaying traffic to and from favorited nodes. Review the device-role documentation before using it. Keep outdoor equipment appropriately enclosed and powered.

Should I use Router mode or increase the hop limit?

Most people should use Client or Client Mute. Client already relays traffic; Router is not a range-boost switch. Infrastructure roles need careful placement and coordination with the local community.

A higher hop limit cannot fix a missing first radio link. It allows a packet to travel through more relays and can add traffic. Start with the BayMesh recommendations and troubleshoot placement before changing them.

Why does Discord show messages that my radio never receives?

The logger collects messages uploaded by gateways across a much larger area than your radio may reach. Seeing a message there means a gateway heard it, not that a radio path exists back to your node. Hills, disconnected groups of nodes, and one-way links can all affect what you receive.

MQTT connects radios to internet services. It is optional and does not replace a working local radio path for off-grid communication.

Why isn’t my node on the map? Are all map entries reachable?

Public maps depend on nodes sharing positions and that data reaching the map service, often through MQTT. A node may be active without publishing its location. Conversely, a map entry or your app’s node list may contain old information.

Check when a node was last heard and whether it was heard over radio or via MQTT. Seeing a distant node in the list does not prove you can reach it now.

Are my messages and location private?

Treat the default channel as public: its encryption key is published. Messages may also appear in internet-connected loggers. The OK to MQTT setting is a forwarding preference, not a way to keep public radio traffic secret.

For a private group, create a channel with a random shared key and keep its QR code private. Review location sharing separately on every enabled channel; a private chat doesn’t automatically hide positions broadcast on the public channel. Our private-use guide walks through both options. See also the official encryption overview.

Which firmware should I install? Why won’t Bluetooth reconnect?

Use the current Beta release recommended for your exact device unless you are deliberately testing Alpha firmware. Read the release notes and follow the official flashing instructions; the update method depends on the hardware. Keep a private backup of your channel settings before updating.

After a reflash or reset, you may need to forget the old Bluetooth pairing in your phone’s settings and pair again. Check the device-specific instructions if pairing still fails. Firmware updates do not travel over the LoRa mesh. See the official firmware FAQ.

Can I rely on Meshtastic in an emergency?

It can be another way to reach people, provided your radios are charged and there is a working radio path. Practice from real locations, agree on check-in times, and keep another communication plan. Neither the public mesh nor a private channel guarantees delivery or connects you to emergency services. See Emergency and Private Use.

Where can I get help or find people to test with?

Join BayMesh on Discord or take part in the weekly net. When asking for help, include your device model, firmware version, general area, radio settings, and what you have already tried. Remove private channel keys and precise home locations from screenshots.

3 - Getting Started

A starting point for new Meshtastic users

Start with the official Meshtastic getting started guide if you haven’t read it yet.

Getting started

  1. Get a supported device. See our hardware recommendations.
  2. Check the firmware and update if needed. Use the Meshtastic Web Flasher and follow the instructions for your exact device; a routine update does not always require a full erase.
  3. Connect to the node from your phone or computer using Bluetooth or a serial connection.
  4. Apply our recommended settings.
  5. Join us on Discord and send a message.

3.1 - Meshtastic Basics

How Meshtastic devices exchange messages over a mesh network

Meshtastic lets you exchange short messages without cellular service or Wi-Fi. It runs on small, low-power radios that use LoRa to reach nearby devices. Each device is called a node. Your phone usually connects to your node over Bluetooth; the nodes communicate with one another over radio.

How the mesh works

When you send a message, nearby nodes can relay it across the mesh. If one node goes offline, a message may still travel through other nodes within range. Delivery depends on a working radio path and compatible settings. Terrain, buildings, antennas, and node placement all affect range; several kilometers is possible with a clear path.

Meshtastic firmware handles radio communication and message routing. The Android and iOS apps, along with a web interface, let you configure your node and send messages. Many devices can run on batteries for extended periods. Our hardware recommendations cover portable radios, DIY kits, and home nodes.

Channels and privacy

A channel gives a group shared settings for exchanging messages. Channels can use encryption, but the default public channel uses a key known to everyone. For a private group, generate a random key and share it only with members. See Emergency and Private Use for setup and location-sharing guidance.

Where to start

Meshtastic can help hikers, neighbors, and event groups communicate where cell coverage is unavailable. It can also be one part of an emergency plan, though messages need a working radio path and are not guaranteed to arrive.

Start with the getting-started guide: choose a compatible device, install its firmware, connect with the app or web interface, and apply the BayMesh settings. Then test messages from the places where you plan to use it.

3.2 - MQTT

How to connect a stationary node to Bay Area MQTT

MQTT is an advanced feature. If you’re unfamiliar with it, leave it disabled. You do not need to enable MQTT on your own node for your messages to appear in the logger.

MQTT is most useful on base stations that can receive mesh traffic and have a stable internet connection.

MQTT sends mesh data to internet services such as mesh maps and the Discord logger. Setting it up is optional. Use it only on a stationary node with a reliable internet connection, such as a rooftop node. Portable nodes need no additional setup.

See the official MQTT module documentation.

MQTT Settings

To use MQTT, configure the following settings:

  • Module Configuration > MQTT

    • MQTT Enabled: Enabled
    • Address: mqtt.bayme.sh
      • Our Bay Area MQTT server
    • Username: meshdev
    • Password: large4cats
    • Encryption Enabled: Enabled
    • JSON Enabled: Enabled
      • This works on ESP32 devices, but not RAK devices.
      • It has limited use and is not needed by the logger or Meshview.
    • TLS Enabled: Optional
      • TLS is supported if you want to use it.
    • Root Topic: msh/US/bayarea
      • Enter this exactly; capitalization matters.
    • Proxy to client enabled: Disabled
      • This sends MQTT messages through your phone’s internet connection instead of the node’s connection.
      • Logging this way requires your phone to stay near the node, so it is likely to be unreliable.
    • Map Report Enabled: Optional
      • This reports your node’s location along with other network data.

  • Radio Configuration > Channels > 0 / Primary

    • Uplink enabled: Enabled
    • Downlink enabled: Disabled
      • Keep downlink disabled. It sends messages from the MQTT server onto the mesh, increasing traffic and potentially degrading performance.

Our picks for a first node, a portable radio, or a home base station

Start with the kind of node you’ll use: something to carry, a kit to build, or a base station to leave at home. For BayMesh, choose hardware and an antenna suitable for the US 902–928 MHz band, usually sold as 915 MHz, and select Meshtastic firmware when ordering.

These picks cover a few useful starting points. For more local advice, visit the Discord resources thread. Once your radio arrives, follow our recommended settings.

  • Good fit: A first node, walks, hikes, and everyday use.
  • Highlights: Compact, lightweight, built-in GPS, and about $40 before shipping.
  • Tradeoffs: No screen or external antenna connector. It uses a magnetic charging/data cable, so keep that cable handy. Battery life depends on GPS use, settings, and mesh traffic.

3.4 - Recommended Settings

Recommended settings for joining the Bay Area mesh

Check your device’s firmware before you get started. The Meshtastic Web Flasher provides device-specific update instructions. Use the current recommended Beta release for your device; Alpha releases are for testing. Read the release notes before updating.

Set automatic telemetry and position broadcasts from stationary nodes to intervals of at least six hours. If you want to share your position while moving, use Smart Position with a minimum interval of 10 minutes and a distance trigger of 100–130 meters. See the Smart Position settings for the exact controls. These settings reduce unnecessary background traffic on the mesh.

If you’re joining our network, you can fill out the optional registration form. You can also visit a Discord logger channel and enter /linknode !nodeid.

For the Bay Area mesh, review all of the following settings:

  • LoRa
    • Set region to United States (US)
    • Preset: Medium Range Fast
    • Frequency slot: 45. Set it explicitly to stay on the BayMesh frequency even if you later rename your primary channel.
    • Number of hops for an infrastructure node: 4 (the default is 3; up to 5 is acceptable)
    • Number of hops for a personal or chat node: 6 (up to 7 if desired)
    • Ignore MQTT: Optional. Enable this to ignore traffic downlinked from MQTT (the internet).
    • OK to MQTT: Optional. Enable this to allow your messages to be uploaded to MQTT. For the public channel, participating gateways use this permission to forward traffic to the Discord logger, Meshview, and maps. It does not guarantee that a gateway will hear you, and it is not a privacy control for public messages.
  • Channels
    • 0 Primary Channel
      • Name: Blank (default)
      • Key: AQ== (default)
      • Turn off Positions & Location if you don’t want to broadcast your location.
      • Review the channel’s position precision before sharing a location. The default public key is known to everyone; see private-use guidance for private groups.
  • User
    • Long Name: Choose something descriptive, such as your name, Discord handle, or amateur radio call sign. You can also include an email address or website. Tip: Include BayMe.sh so more people can find us.
    • Short Name: Use up to four characters that distinguish this radio from your other nodes. This name appears in chat; emoji are welcome.
    • Licensed Operator: Leave this off to join BayMesh, even if you hold an amateur radio license. Read Licensed Operations before using ham mode.
  • Device Config
    • Device Role
      • Client Mute — Best for vehicles or when you have more than two nodes in one place. It transmits its own packets but does not relay messages.
      • Client — Recommended for a home base station or your single primary node. It relays messages it receives.
      • Router / Repeater — Do not use these roles. They require additional care, and the Client role already relays messages.
      • See the official guide to device roles.
  • Module Configuration
    • MQTT
      • For a stationary gateway with a reliable internet connection, see MQTT. You do not need MQTT enabled on your portable node to use the mesh or have a nearby gateway upload your public messages.
    • Device Telemetry (iOS)
      • Device Metrics: 6 hours (iOS) / 21,600 seconds (Android)
      • Sensor (Environment) Metrics: 6 hours / 21,600 seconds (Android)
    • Neighbor Info
      • Neighbor Info is optional and does not improve routing. Current firmware restricts sending it over LoRa on the default public channel.
      • If you enable it for a supported setup, use an update interval of 4 hours / 14,400 seconds. See the official module documentation for availability and controls.

5 - Emergency and Private Use

Set up a private group and practice communicating without cellular service

Meshtastic can help friends, families, and neighbors exchange short messages without cellular service. You can hold private conversations on the community mesh or build a separate mesh for your group. Decide which approach you need before changing your settings.

Private conversations on BayMesh

Keep the BayMesh radio settings: US region, Medium Fast preset, and frequency slot 45. Leave the public primary channel in place, then add a secondary channel with a custom name and a newly generated random encryption key. Give each group member the same channel name and key using the app’s channel-sharing link or QR code.

Treat the link or QR code like a password: it contains the key. The default public key, AQ==, is known to everyone and does not make a conversation private. Use the app’s key generator, preferably for a 256-bit key, instead of a memorable word. See the official channel configuration and encryption guide.

Your group shares radio airtime with BayMesh. Other nodes may relay your encrypted packets without being able to read the messages. A private channel does not reserve capacity or guarantee delivery.

A separate mesh for your group

For a group that wants to operate separately from BayMesh:

  1. Set every radio to the US region and the same modem preset. Start with the Client role so your nodes can relay for one another.
  2. Give the primary channel a custom name and generate a random encryption key. Share those settings privately with every member.
  3. Set Frequency Slot to 0 (automatic) if you want Meshtastic to choose a slot based on the primary channel name. Changing only the encryption key does not change the frequency. Renaming the primary channel affects automatic slot selection; it does not override a manually selected slot.
  4. Check the resulting frequency. A custom name can still select the same slot as another mesh. To operate separately, choose an available valid slot other than BayMesh’s 45 for Medium Fast, and explicitly set it on every radio. Ask in Discord if you need help coordinating locally.
  5. Test messages in both directions before separating the radios.

The official LoRa configuration guide explains automatic and manual slot selection. A separate frequency means you cannot count on BayMesh nodes to relay your messages. Your group needs its own connected radio path.

Keep location and internet sharing intentional

Review position sharing on every enabled channel, including the public primary channel. A private secondary chat does not automatically hide location broadcasts sent elsewhere. Disable location sharing where you don’t need it, or choose an appropriate precision. See Meshtastic’s location-sharing guidance.

For a radio-only group, disable MQTT and channel uplinks/downlinks on your nodes. Keep your private keys out of public screenshots and support requests. If a radio or key is lost, generate a new group key and update the remaining radios.

Practice before an outage

  • Test from the places people will actually use their radios: home, work, meeting points, and likely travel routes. Hills and buildings can break the path between nearby addresses.
  • Agree on check-in times, short message formats, and a meeting plan if no reply arrives. Ask for a written reply when confirmation matters.
  • Keep radios and phones charged, with suitable spare power and charging cables. Test the app with cellular data and Wi-Fi off and Bluetooth on.
  • Save the setup instructions and private channel QR code somewhere your group can access offline. Practice restoring a spare radio.

BayMesh is a volunteer network, and coverage, power, and message delivery can change during an outage. Meshtastic does not contact 911 or a monitored rescue service; use it as one part of your communication plan.

6 - Licensed Operations

How amateur-radio operation differs from joining the public BayMesh network

You do not need an amateur radio license to join BayMesh with appropriate US-region hardware operated within applicable limits. If you have a call sign, you can use it as your node name while leaving Licensed Operator off and following our recommended settings.

Licensed Operator changes how your station operates. Enable it only when you intend to operate under amateur radio rules. The guidance below concerns US operation.

What changes in amateur-radio mode?

Amateur operation can support experiments with higher transmit power and different antenna systems. It also brings different responsibilities:

  • Messages must not be encrypted to conceal their meaning. That includes a private family channel. The relevant restriction is 47 CFR § 97.113(a)(4).
  • Identify your station. Set your long name to your call sign. US rules require identification at the end of a communication and at least every 10 minutes during a communication; make sure your setup meets those requirements. See § 97.119.
  • Observe power limits. For spread-spectrum emissions, § 97.313(j) limits transmitter output to 10 W PEP. The same section requires using only the power needed for communication. This is not a recommendation to run 10 W, and the radio’s advertised output does not establish what your installation may legally use.
  • Keep the activity appropriate for amateur radio. Business traffic and other prohibited transmissions remain subject to § 97.113.

The official Meshtastic amateur radio FAQ explains why licensed mode and the normal encrypted public mesh are not interoperable. A node in licensed mode cannot participate in BayMesh’s public channel.

Setting up a licensed experiment

Coordinate with the other operators first. Agree on an appropriate frequency, preset, unencrypted channel, and test schedule. Keep experiments separate from the public mesh and follow the band privileges, station-control requirements, and interference rules that apply to your station.

In the app’s User settings, set Long Name to your call sign and enable Licensed Operator (also called Is Licensed). Review the channel configuration and confirm that encryption is disabled. Check your firmware’s behavior and identification settings; a single toggle does not address every operating requirement. The official user configuration guide describes the controls.

Check the antenna, feed line, power supply, cooling, and RF-exposure requirements before increasing output power. A better location often helps a link more than additional power, especially when the other end is a small handheld.

Returning to BayMesh

Disable Licensed Operator and restore the public mesh’s recommended settings, including the primary channel name and key, US region, Medium Fast preset, and slot 45. Restore suitable transmit-power settings, including any hardware power jumpers, before transmitting. Confirm you can exchange messages with another BayMesh node.

For private family or neighborhood messaging, use the regular encrypted mode described in Emergency and Private Use.
