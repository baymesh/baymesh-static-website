Getting Started

From opening the box to your first reply on BayMesh
  • 1: Meshtastic Basics
  • 2: MQTT
  • 3: Recommended Hardware
  • 4: Recommended Settings

You don’t need to be a radio expert to join BayMesh. Start with one supported radio, the Meshtastic app, and a place to test. This guide takes you from a new device to a conversation.

1. Get the radio ready

If you’re still choosing, start with our hardware recommendations. Buy the version intended for the US 915 MHz band and check whether the package includes a battery, case, and antenna.

Charge the radio with the cable and power supply specified by its manufacturer. If it has a removable antenna, attach the correct antenna before powering it on. Devices such as the T1000-E have a built-in antenna.

Check whether Meshtastic is already installed. If it needs an update, use the recommended Beta release for that exact model and follow the official flashing instructions. A routine update doesn’t always require a full erase.

2. Install the app and pair

Install the official Meshtastic app while you still have internet access. The Android download page and Apple download page link to the available stores. Turn on Bluetooth and keep the radio near your phone.

PhoneConnect to your radio
AndroidOpen Meshtastic, allow the Bluetooth or Nearby Devices permissions it requests, and open Connect. Scan for Bluetooth devices, select your radio, and accept the pairing prompt. Some Android versions also require location permission for scanning.
iPhone / iPadOpen Meshtastic, allow Bluetooth access, and tap Connect. Select your radio from the nearby devices list and complete pairing.

If asked for a PIN, use the one shown by the radio or specified in its device instructions. Wait until the app shows a connection before changing radio settings. Bluetooth connects your phone to your radio; the separate LoRa link connects that radio to the mesh.

See Android and iPhone setup screenshots

App screens change between releases. The official guides above cover the current controls. If the connection fails, start with Troubleshooting.

3. Apply BayMesh settings

Open Settings → LoRa on Android, or Settings → Radio Configuration → LoRa on Apple devices. Set:

  • Region: United States (US)
  • Modem preset: Medium Fast / Medium Range Fast
  • Frequency slot: Leave it at 0 (automatic) or 45. With the US region, Medium Fast preset, and default primary channel name, automatic selects slot 45, so you probably won’t need to change it.

Keep the default public primary channel: blank name and key AQ==. On a new radio, you shouldn’t need to change the other settings to get started. Save or send your changes; the radio may restart when you change some settings.

Enable OK to MQTT in the LoRa settings if you’d like to see your public messages in Discord or on Meshview. This lets participating gateways upload messages they hear from your radio.

You can also give the radio a recognizable long name and a short name of up to four characters. For more detail, see our recommended settings.

4. Send a first message

Try a clear location, such as an upper-floor window or an outdoor spot. Open the public channel in the app and send a short introduction:

Hi BayMesh! New node in Oakland near Lake Merritt. Can someone reply if you hear me?

Use a general neighborhood, not your exact home address. Give people time to reply. If nobody answers, arrange a test in Discord or join the weekly net.

An acknowledgment is useful, but a written reply is the best first test of a conversation. Receiving a distant node doesn’t prove it can hear you back. If you see traffic but can’t get a reply, work through Troubleshooting before sending more test messages.

5. Try the places you care about

Once you have a successful exchange, try the rooms, walks, or travel routes where you’ll use your radio. You can test without cellular data or Wi-Fi; keep Bluetooth on so the app stays connected to the radio.

For better reception at home, read Home Base Station. For family check-ins, start with Emergency Preparedness and Private Channels and Groups.

You’re welcome to ask questions and learn as you go. Keep politics off the community channels, be kind, and help the next person get their first reply too.

1 - Meshtastic Basics

How Meshtastic devices exchange messages over a mesh network

Meshtastic lets you exchange short messages without cellular service or Wi-Fi. It runs on small, low-power radios that use LoRa to reach nearby devices. Each device is called a node. Your phone usually connects to your node over Bluetooth; the nodes communicate with one another over radio.

How the mesh works

When you send a message, nearby nodes can relay it across the mesh. If one node goes offline, a message may still travel through other nodes within range. Delivery depends on a working radio path and compatible settings. Terrain, buildings, antennas, and node placement all affect range; several kilometers is possible with a clear path.

Meshtastic firmware handles radio communication and message routing. The Android and iOS apps, along with a web interface, let you configure your node and send messages. Many devices can run on batteries for extended periods. Our hardware recommendations cover portable radios, DIY kits, and home nodes.

Channels and privacy

A channel gives a group shared settings for exchanging messages. Channels can use encryption, but the default public channel uses a key known to everyone. For a private group, generate a random key and share it only with members. See Private Channels and Groups for setup and location-sharing guidance.

Where to start

Meshtastic can help hikers, neighbors, and event groups communicate where cell coverage is unavailable. It can also be one part of an emergency plan, though messages need a working radio path and are not guaranteed to arrive.

Start with the getting-started guide: choose a compatible device, install its firmware, connect with the app or web interface, and apply the BayMesh settings. Then test messages from the places where you plan to use it.

2 - MQTT

How to connect a stationary node to Bay Area MQTT

MQTT is an advanced feature. If you’re unfamiliar with it, leave it disabled. You do not need to enable MQTT on your own node for your messages to appear in the logger.

MQTT is most useful on base stations that can receive mesh traffic and have a stable internet connection.

MQTT sends mesh data to internet services such as mesh maps and the Discord logger. Setting it up is optional. Use it only on a stationary node with a reliable internet connection, such as a rooftop node. Portable nodes need no additional setup.

See the official MQTT module documentation.

MQTT Settings

To use MQTT, configure the following settings:

  • Module Configuration > MQTT

    • MQTT Enabled: Enabled
    • Address: mqtt.bayme.sh
      • Our Bay Area MQTT server
    • Username: meshdev
    • Password: large4cats
    • Encryption Enabled: Enabled
    • TLS Enabled: Optional
      • TLS is supported if you want to use it.
    • Root Topic: msh/US/bayarea
      • Enter this exactly; capitalization matters.
    • Proxy to client enabled: Disabled
      • This sends MQTT messages through your phone’s internet connection instead of the node’s connection.
      • Logging this way requires your phone to stay near the node, so it is likely to be unreliable.
    • Map Report Enabled: Optional
      • This reports your node’s location along with other network data.

  • Radio Configuration > Channels > 0 / Primary

    • Uplink enabled: Enabled
    • Downlink enabled: Disabled
      • Keep downlink disabled. It sends messages from the MQTT server onto the mesh, increasing traffic and potentially degrading performance.

3 - Recommended Hardware

Our picks for a first node, a portable radio, or a home base station

Start with the kind of node you’ll use: something to carry, a kit to build, or a base station to leave at home. For BayMesh, choose hardware and an antenna suitable for the US 902–928 MHz band, usually sold as 915 MHz, and select Meshtastic firmware when ordering.

These picks cover a few useful starting points. For more local advice, visit the Discord resources thread. Once your radio arrives, follow our recommended settings.

Seeed Studio SenseCAP Card Tracker T1000-E

Our first pick for a simple, portable starter node. The T1000-E packs GPS, a 700 mAh battery, and an internal antenna into a slim card-shaped case. Its IP65 rating makes it a practical everyday carry, and there’s no assembly required. Use your phone to read and send messages.

  • Good fit: A first node, walks, hikes, and everyday use.
  • Highlights: Compact, lightweight, built-in GPS, and about $40 before shipping.
  • Tradeoffs: No screen or external antenna connector. It uses a magnetic charging/data cable, so keep that cable handy. Battery life depends on GPS use, settings, and mesh traffic.

  • Good fit: Tinkering, custom enclosures, and DIY home or solar nodes.

Our pick for a powered home base station. With 1 watt (1,000 mW) of transmit power for US operation, the Station G3 is more powerful than any other radio on this page. Its modular design includes an ESP32-S3 host, an OLED display, an SMA antenna connector, and an adjustable receive amplifier. The receive settings can help you adapt it to a quiet or noisy radio environment.

  • Good fit: A fixed installation with a good antenna location and reliable external power.
  • Highlights: 1-watt transmit power, replaceable boards, and adjustable radio hardware.
  • Tradeoffs: No built-in battery. It needs a separate 15 V USB-C PD or 9–19 V DC supply rated at least 25 W; an ordinary USB phone charger may not work. Choose the complete node, rather than a motherboard or RF-board bundle.

4 - Recommended Settings

Join BayMesh, choose the right device role, and share the airwaves considerately

These are BayMesh’s local settings. Apply them to each radio you want to use on the community mesh, then test messages in both directions. Start with Getting Started if you haven’t paired your radio yet.

Choose a descriptive Long Name, such as your name or Discord handle, and a Short Name of up to four characters. Give each of your radios a distinct name. Only put contact details in the name if you’re comfortable making them public.

A vehicle does not automatically need Client Mute: a single mobile node can still be a useful Client. Choose Mute when another nearby node already provides the relay you need. Router mode is not a range boost, and a solar panel or rooftop does not by itself justify an infrastructure role. Client Base is our recommendation for a home solar base station.

Client Base gives special relay treatment to traffic to and from its favorited nodes; it handles other traffic like a Client. Favoriting belongs on the base radio, not just on your handheld. See the official device-role reference.

Hop limits

These are the existing BayMesh recommendations, rather than the firmware defaults:

Node useHop limit
Stationary relay, including a home Client Base4; up to 5 where needed
Personal or chat node6; up to 7 where needed

The firmware default is 3. A hop limit allows traffic through a certain number of relays; it doesn’t increase your radio’s transmit power. More hops can add traffic, so don’t raise the limit just because a test failed. First establish a working link to another node. See Troubleshooting.

Position and telemetry

Share only the location detail you want others to see. Turn off position sharing on the public channel if you don’t want to publish your location, or choose an appropriate position precision. Check every enabled channel: adding a private channel doesn’t hide broadcasts on the public one. See Private Channels and Groups.

Automatic updateRecommended interval
Stationary position reportsAt least 6 hours
Device and environmental metricsAt least 6 hours (21,600 seconds)
Position while moving, if wantedSmart Position with a minimum interval of 10 minutes and a distance trigger of 100–130 meters

These intervals keep background broadcasts from crowding out conversations. The distance setting is in meters; see the Smart Position controls.

Neighbor Info is optional and does not improve routing. Current firmware restricts sending it over LoRa on the default public channel. If your setup supports it and you enable it, use an interval of 4 hours / 14,400 seconds. See the module documentation.

Internet sharing and MQTT

You don’t need MQTT on your portable radio to use BayMesh.

  • OK to MQTT: Enable this in the LoRa settings if you want your public messages to appear in Discord or on Meshview. It lets participating gateways upload messages they hear from you, which means anyone on the internet can see those messages. A gateway still needs to hear your radio. Leaving it off does not make the public radio channel private.
  • Ignore MQTT: Optional. Enable it to ignore traffic downlinked from the internet.
  • Run a gateway: For a stationary node with a reliable internet connection, follow our MQTT guide.

Share the mesh

BayMesh brings together people from across the Bay Area. The world can be divisive; on the mesh, we’re one big happy mesh. Keep politics and campaigning off the community channels. Welcome newcomers, be patient with questions, and treat one another with respect.

  • Make tests useful. Send a short introduction with your general area and ask for a reply. Give people time to respond before trying again.
  • Use traceroute sparingly. Run it to answer a specific question, then wait. Repeated requests and automated tests consume shared airtime.
  • Keep broadcasts modest. Use the position and telemetry intervals above, and avoid rapid beacons or repeated status messages.
  • Coordinate infrastructure. Talk with the community before changing shared nodes or adding a Router. A well-placed Client can already help.
  • Keep private details private. Public messages may reach internet loggers. Don’t post channel keys, someone else’s precise location, or personal information without permission.

Keep in touch and stay current

Join Discord to arrange a test or ask for help. You can fill out the optional registration form, or enter /linknode !nodeid in a Discord logger channel to link your node.

Use the current recommended Beta firmware for your exact device; Alpha releases are for testing. Read the release notes, keep a private backup of your settings, and follow the official flashing instructions.
